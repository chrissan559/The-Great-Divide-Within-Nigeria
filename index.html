The Great Divide: Why Some Nigerians Rise While Others Work Harder and Sink

By Kaizen Chris

The dividing line isn't the industry. It's whether you own the asset, earn in hard currency, and control distribution, or rent out your time in naira.

Two people can finish the same school, work equally hard, and end up in different worlds. The difference is rarely intelligence or character. It's which side of the system they stand on.

The three tests

  • CurrencyAre you paid in dollars or naira? Dollars protect you, naira erodes.
  • OwnershipDo you own the thing, or just work on it?
  • Scale and accessCan you reach credit, contracts, and connections that make money grow faster?

Part 1: Skills

Rich getting richer

  • Software, AI, and data work sold to foreign clientsA developer in Port Harcourt who builds websites for a client in Canada earns in dollars. When the naira falls, their income rises in naira terms without them doing anything extra.
  • Sales and distributionSomeone who can sell, or who controls how goods reach buyers, earns commissions that rise with prices. Sales is one of the few skills where income grows when the economy gets harder.
  • Finance and dealmakingPeople who structure deals, raise money, or connect buyers to sellers get a cut of large transactions. A small percentage of a big deal beats a full salary.
  • CybersecurityEvery bank, fintech, and company needs protection from hackers, and the skill is scarce, so employers pay well, often in dollars.

Poor getting poorer

  • Entry-level clerical workTyping, filing, and basic office admin have thousands of applicants for every opening, so pay stays low.
  • Low-paid salaried jobs like civil service and teachingThese are respectable and important jobs. The problem is that the salary is fixed in naira while rent, food, and transport keep rising. A teacher on ₦70,000 feels poorer every year without a pay cut.
  • Manual laborBricklaying, loading, and factory floor work pay by the day. If you can't work, you don't earn, and the pay rarely keeps up with prices.
  • Any skill sold only for local naira with many competitorsEven a good skill, like graphic design or writing, becomes a poverty trap if you only sell it locally where everyone undercuts each other.

Part 2: Businesses

Rich getting richer

  • Real estate and landLand almost never loses value. Someone who bought a plot in Lekki or Abuja years ago for a few million may now hold something worth many times more, while renters kept paying.
  • Fintech and paymentsEvery transfer, POS transaction, and bill payment pays the platform a small fee. Millions of small fees add up to huge income for the owners.
  • Energy, oil, and gasFuel, gas, and power are things everyone must buy, and they're priced in or tied to dollars.
  • TelecomEveryone needs data and airtime. Owners collect from millions of people monthly.
  • BankingBanks borrow cheap and lend at high rates. They profit whether the economy is good or bad.
  • Large-scale manufacturingMaking goods in volume (cement, drinks, packaged food) lowers the cost per unit and lets owners undercut small competitors.
  • LogisticsMoving goods between cities and ports is essential, and owners of fleets and warehouses earn from every delivery.
  • Import and exportSomeone with access to dollars who imports in bulk gets better prices and sells to smaller traders at a markup.
  • Agribusiness at scaleA big farm with processing and storage earns well because it can hold produce until prices rise. A small farmer must sell right after harvest when prices are lowest.

Poor getting poorer

  • Petty tradingSelling small quantities of goods leaves thin profit, and one bad price change can wipe out capital.
  • Small shops with rising restocking costsThe shop owner sells a carton of noodles today at last month's price, then discovers restocking costs more. The profit is gone before the goods are sold.
  • Roadside foodGas, oil, rice, and pepper all cost more, but customers won't pay much more, so margins shrink.
  • TailoringFabric, thread, and electricity costs rise, while customers compare prices and bargain. Cheap imported clothes compete too.
  • Okada, keke, ride-hail, and delivery drivers who rent the vehicle or depend on a platform they don't ownA driver who rents pays the owner daily whether he makes money or not. A platform can cut fares or raise its commission, and the driver has no say. Meanwhile, someone who owns three kekes earns even while resting.

Part 3: Systems

Rich getting richer

  • Access to dollarsPeople who can get dollars at official rates, or hold them, are protected while others buy at higher market prices.
  • Cheap bank creditA big company borrows at a lower rate with collateral and connections. A market woman borrows from a cooperative or loan app at a much higher cost.
  • Political connections and government contractsOne contract can be worth more than a small business earns in a lifetime. Knowing the right person opens doors that hard work alone doesn't.
  • Land titlesPapers such as a Certificate of Occupancy let you use land as collateral, sell it safely, and avoid disputes. Without papers, land is risky and hard to borrow against.
  • Diaspora incomeA family with a relative abroad receives dollars, pounds, or euros regularly, often used to buy land or start a business back home.
  • Private power, water, and securitySolar, a borehole, and a security team protect owners from failing public services and cut their daily costs.

Poor getting poorer

  • Naira devaluation and inflationSavings in a bank account slowly lose value. ₦500,000 saved a few years ago buys much less now.
  • Food and fuel price spikesThe poor spend most of their income on food and transport, so price jumps hit them hardest.
  • No access to affordable creditWithout collateral or connections, people borrow at high interest or not at all, so they can't grow.
  • Generator and other self-provided infrastructure costsA poor household buying fuel, paying for water, and topping up meters spends a larger share of income on things that should be public services.
  • Weak public education and healthcareA family that can't afford private schools or hospitals gets less education and worse health, and a single medical emergency can wipe out years of savings.
  • Brain drainSkilled people like nurses, doctors, engineers, and developers leave for better pay abroad. Those left behind face fewer skilled professionals and weaker services.

The same field can land in either group

Field Poor side Rich side
Developer Paid in naira by a local employer Paid in dollars by global clients, or owns a product
Rider or driver Rents the vehicle or depends on the platform Owns a fleet of vehicles
Farmer Sells at harvest to middlemen Stores, processes, and sells at better prices
Trader Buys small and sells small Imports or buys in bulk and supplies other traders
Teacher Fixed salary only Salary plus an online tutoring business or paid content
Houseowner Rents and pays rising rent Owns property and collects rent

If you're starting with no money

  1. Learn a skill that can be paid in hard currencySoftware, design, writing, video editing, digital marketing, data, or cybersecurity. Free and cheap online resources exist.
  2. Sell to people outside NigeriaEven small freelance jobs pay in dollars, and that difference in currency matters a lot.
  3. Own something, however smallA product, template, audience, tool, or service that keeps earning after the work is done.
  4. Turn income into assetsLand, equipment, dollar savings, or a stake in a business, not naira sitting idle.
  5. Avoid expensive debtHigh-interest loans and borrowing for lifestyle keep you on the poor side.
  6. Guard your time and attentionHours lost to TikTok, betting, and endless entertainment are hours not spent building the skill that moves you across.

Final thought

The system doesn't reward the hardest worker. It rewards whoever holds assets, earns in strong currency, and has access. You can't change the whole system overnight, but you can change which side of it you stand on.
